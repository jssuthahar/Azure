## Start with a question

<Story question="When your phone suggests the next word as you type 'Happy birth…', how does it know you mean 'birthday'?">

It's seen that pattern millions of times. "Happy birth" is almost always followed by "day".

A generative AI model does the same thing at a vastly bigger scale. It doesn't just guess one word for a text message. It predicts the next piece of text, then the next, then the next, until it has written a whole email, a poem or a Python function. Understanding that one idea explains most of what these models do well, and the mistakes they make.

</Story>

## Explain it like I'm 10

<Eli10>

Imagine a friend who has read every storybook in the library.

You start a sentence: "Once upon a time, a little dragon…" and your friend says the next word that fits best. Then the next. Word by word, a whole story appears.

Your friend is great at sounding right. But if you ask about something that wasn't in the books, they might make up something that sounds true. That's why you sometimes hand them the **right book** first and say "answer from this".

</Eli10>

### 1. Tokens

Models don't read words, they read **tokens**. A token can be a whole word ("cat"), part of a word ("un" + "believ" + "able") or punctuation. In English, one token is about **four characters** on average, so 100 tokens is roughly 75 words. Hindi or Tamil text usually needs more tokens for the same meaning. Tokens matter because you **pay per token** (input and output) and because limits are counted in tokens.

### 2. Embeddings

Each token is turned into an **embedding**: a long list of numbers (a vector). The model learns these so that words with related meanings have similar vectors. "King" sits near "queen", and "invoice" sits near "bill". Embedding models in Foundry produce vectors for whole sentences or documents too, which is how semantic search finds "money back" when you searched "refund".

### 3. Transformers and attention

Modern language models use the **transformer** architecture. Its key idea is **attention**: for every token, the model weighs how much each other token matters. In "The bank was closed, so I couldn't deposit the cheque", attention links "bank" with "deposit" and "cheque", so the model treats it as a money bank, not a river bank. Stacking many attention layers lets the model capture grammar, facts and style.

### 4. Next-token prediction

At the end, the model gives a probability to every token it knows. It picks one, adds it to the text and runs again. That loop is the whole "writing" process. It's why answers stream in word by word.

### 5. Temperature and other settings

**Temperature** (0 to 2) controls how the next token is picked. Low temperature nearly always picks the top choice, so answers are focused and repeatable. High temperature takes more chances, so answers vary more. **Top_p** is another way to control randomness; change one or the other, not both. **Max output tokens** caps the answer length. Reasoning models use a reasoning effort setting instead of temperature.

| Task | Temperature |
| --- | --- |
| Answering from a policy, writing code, extracting facts | Low (around 0 to 0.3) |
| Everyday chat and summaries | Middle (around 0.7) |
| Brainstorming slogans or story ideas | Higher (around 1 or more) |

### 6. Context window

The **context window** is the most tokens the model can handle in one request: your system message, the chat history, any grounding documents **and** the answer. If a conversation grows past it, older messages have to be dropped or summarised, and the model "forgets" them.

### 7. Hallucination and grounding

Because the model predicts likely text, it can produce an answer that sounds right but is false. That's a **hallucination**. The main fix is **grounding**: put trusted information in the prompt so the model answers from it. **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** does this automatically: it uses embeddings to find the most relevant chunks of your documents and adds them to the prompt. Add a system message like "answer only from the provided sources", a low temperature and citations, and hallucinations drop sharply.

<Callout type="exam">
Match the symptom to the fix. **Confident but wrong** answers: hallucination, fixed by **grounding / RAG**. **Answers vary too much** between runs: lower the **temperature**. **Replies cut off**: raise **max output tokens**. **Model forgets the start of a long chat**: the **context window** is full. And remember that a model predicts the **next token**; it isn't searching the internet unless a tool lets it.
</Callout>

<DeepDive title="Training versus inference">

**Training** is when the model learns from enormous amounts of text, adjusting billions of internal weights so its next-token guesses get better. It costs a lot and happens once (plus later fine-tuning). **Inference** is when you send a prompt and get an answer; that's what you pay for per token in Foundry. Your prompts at inference time don't change the model's weights, and prompts and completions in Foundry aren't used to train the foundation models. If you want the model to know your data, you ground it at inference time, or in some cases fine-tune it.

</DeepDive>

## When to use it and when not to

<UseWhen
  use={[
    'Writing, summarising, translating or rewriting text where a person can review the result.',
    'Answering questions over your own documents, when you ground the model in them.',
    'Brainstorming and drafting, where variety from a higher temperature helps.',
  ]}
  avoid={[
    'Exact calculations or lookups that a database or code can do perfectly.',
    'Answering from memory about facts that must be correct, with no grounding.',
    'Feeding huge documents in one go without checking the context window and token cost.',
  ]}
