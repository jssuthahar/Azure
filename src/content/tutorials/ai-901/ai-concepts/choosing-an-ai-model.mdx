  - term: Large language model (LLM)
    definition: A model with billions of parameters trained on huge amounts of text, good at open-ended language tasks such as chat, writing and summarising.
  - term: Small language model (SLM)
    definition: A compact language model, such as Microsoft Phi, that costs less and responds faster, and can run on smaller hardware or devices.
  - term: Multimodal model
    definition: A model that accepts more than one kind of input, such as text plus images or audio, for example GPT-4o or GPT-4.1.
  - term: Reasoning model
    definition: A model that spends extra compute working through a problem step by step before answering, such as the o-series models.
  - term: Embedding model
    definition: A model that turns text into a vector of numbers so similar meanings sit close together, used for search and retrieval.
  - term: Model card
    definition: The page in the model catalog that describes a model's purpose, inputs, outputs, limits, license and intended use.

## Start with a question

<Story question="Would you hire a senior architect to change a light bulb?">

You could. They'd do a fine job. But you'd pay architect rates for a five-minute task, and you'd wait until they had a free slot.

AI models work the same way. The biggest model can do almost anything, but it's slower and costs more per request. Picking a model means finding the **smallest, cheapest one that still does the job well**, and switching to a specialist when the job needs one.

</Story>

## Explain it like I'm 10

<Eli10>

Think of a toolbox. A big multi-tool can do lots of things, but it's heavy.

A small screwdriver is light and quick, perfect for one kind of screw.

A camera sees pictures. A paintbrush makes new pictures. A calculator works through hard sums slowly and carefully.

You pick the tool that fits the job. AI models are those tools.

</Eli10>

## The technical explanation

Microsoft Foundry's **model catalog** holds thousands of models: Microsoft's **Phi** family, OpenAI models (GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, the GPT-5 family, o-series reasoning models, gpt-image-1), Meta Llama, Mistral, DeepSeek, Cohere and more. Some are **Foundry Models sold by Azure** (billed and supported by Microsoft); others come from partners and the community.

Start with what the model has to *do*, because that rules most models out straight away.

| Model type | What it does | Example job | Example models |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Large language model (LLM) | Broad, open-ended text: chat, writing, summarising, code | A general helpdesk assistant | GPT-4.1, GPT-5 family, Llama |
| Small language model (SLM) | Narrower text tasks, cheaper and faster, can run on smaller hardware | Classify support tickets, run on a field device | Phi |
| Multimodal model | Takes text plus images (and sometimes audio) as input | Read a photo of a damaged parcel and describe the damage | GPT-4o, GPT-4.1 |
| Reasoning model | Works through a problem step by step before answering | Plan a delivery route, solve a pricing puzzle, debug code | o-series |
| Embedding model | Turns text into vectors that capture meaning | Semantic search, the retrieval step in RAG | text-embedding models |
| Image-generation model | Creates or edits images from a text prompt | A festival poster for a shop | gpt-image-1 |
| Speech or audio model | Takes audio in or produces audio out | A voice assistant | gpt-audio family, gpt-realtime |

### Reading a model card and benchmarks

Every model in the catalog has a **model card**. It tells you the provider, what the model is meant for, the inputs and outputs it supports (text, image, audio), the **context window** (how many tokens it can read at once), languages, known limits and the **license**. The catalog also lets you filter by task, collection, deployment type and license.

**Benchmarks** (the leaderboards) compare models on **quality**, **cost** and **throughput**. They're a good shortlist tool. They aren't the final answer, because a model that wins a public benchmark can still do badly on your invoices or your Hindi customer emails. Deploy two or three candidates and test them with real prompts.

### The trade-off triangle

Most decisions come down to three things:

- **Quality**: how good and accurate the answers are.
- **Cost**: you pay per token, and bigger models charge more per token.
- **Latency**: how long the user waits. Bigger and reasoning models are slower.

You rarely get all three at the top. A common pattern is to send easy requests to a small model and hard ones to a larger or reasoning model.

<Callout type="exam">
Questions describe the job and expect you to name the model type. "Find documents with similar meaning" is an **embedding** model. "Answer a question about a photo" is **multimodal**. "Create an image" is **image generation**. "Cheapest and fastest for a simple, narrow task" is a **small language model**. "Solve a multi-step problem" is a **reasoning** model.
</Callout>

<DeepDive title="Routing and fine-tuning">

Teams with high volume often put a router in front: a cheap model handles the bulk and only escalates when the request looks hard. Fine-tuning is another lever. A fine-tuned small model trained on your own examples can match a much larger model on one narrow task at a fraction of the token cost. Check the model card first, because not every model supports fine-tuning, and fine-tuned models have their own deployment options.

</DeepDive>
