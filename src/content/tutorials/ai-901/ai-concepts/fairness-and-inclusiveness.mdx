    q: What's the difference between fairness and inclusiveness?
    a: >-
      Fairness is about outcomes: two similar people should get the same result. Inclusiveness is about who can use the system at all: a voice app that only understands American accents, or a form a screen reader can't read, leaves people out even if its decisions are fair.

    q: How does bias get into an AI model if nobody programs it in?

    q: A speech-to-text app works well in testing but customers in Tamil Nadu say it misses half their words. What went wrong and what would you do?
    a: >-
      It wasn't tested with representative speakers, so it fails an inclusiveness check. I'd collect test audio from the real user base, measure word error rate by accent, and consider a custom speech model trained on those accents. Until then, I'd offer a typing option.

## Start with a question

<Story question="If a company's past hiring was unfair, and you train an AI on that history, what will the AI learn?">

It'll learn to be unfair, very efficiently. The AI doesn't know the history was wrong. It just copies the pattern it sees.

That's why **fairness** is the first of Microsoft's responsible AI principles. Its partner, **inclusiveness**, asks a different question: can everyone use the system at all, whatever their ability, language or accent?

</Story>

## Explain it like I'm 10

<Eli10>

Imagine a teacher who learned what a "good student" looks like only by watching the front row. Now she gives better marks to kids who sit in front, even when the back-row kids write the same answers. That's **unfair**.

Now imagine the class quiz is only read aloud, so a classmate who can't hear well can't take part. That's not **inclusive**.

A fair teacher marks the answers, not the seat. An inclusive teacher gives the quiz on paper too.

</Eli10>

## The technical explanation

### Fairness

An AI system is **fair** when people in similar situations get similar results. Unfairness creeps in through:

| Source of bias | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Skewed training data | A CV screener trained on ten years of mostly male hires |
| Proxy features | Postcode or surname standing in for religion or caste |
| Biased labels | Past "good customer" tags given unevenly by staff |
| Unrepresentative testing | A face model tested mostly on lighter skin tones |
| Generative output | A model that writes "he" for engineers and "she" for nurses |

To reduce it, you collect **representative data**, remove or test proxy features, and run **disaggregated evaluations**: accuracy, approval rate or answer quality measured **for each group**, not only overall. In Microsoft Foundry, evaluations score model answers for quality and include risk and safety evaluators (hateful and unfair content is one). For classic machine learning models, the open-source Fairlearn toolkit and the Responsible AI dashboard in Azure Machine Learning compare results across groups.

### Inclusiveness

An AI system is **inclusive** when it's designed with and for the full range of people who'll use it.

- Speech that understands regional accents, and captions for people who are deaf or hard of hearing.
- Text to speech and screen-reader-friendly output for people who are blind or have low vision.
- Answers in Hindi, Tamil or Marathi, not only English.
- Plain wording for people with low literacy, and voice input for people who can't type.

Microsoft's Seeing AI app is a well-known example: it uses computer vision to describe the world aloud for people with low vision.

<Callout type="exam">
If the scenario mentions a **group getting a worse outcome**, the answer is fairness. If it mentions **people who can't use the system** (disability, language, accent, low literacy), the answer is inclusiveness. Watch for answer options that say "remove the gender column" as the full fix: hidden proxies mean you still have to measure results.
</Callout>

<DeepDive title="Fair to whom? Choosing a fairness measure">

There's more than one definition of fair. Equal approval rates across groups, equal error rates, and equal accuracy among people who truly qualify can't always all be true at once. Teams pick the measure that matches the harm they're trying to avoid (a missed loan versus a wrongly approved one), write down why, and get sign-off from the people accountable. That's where fairness meets accountability.

</DeepDive>

## When to use it and when not to

<UseWhen
  use={[
    'Any AI that affects people\'s access to jobs, loans, housing, healthcare or education. Check fairness before launch and after every model update.',
    'Public-facing apps. Design for disabilities, languages and accents from day one.',
    'Generative AI that writes about people. Test prompts that differ only by name, gender or region.',
  ]}
  avoid={[
    'Assuming a model is fair because it never sees a sensitive column.',
    'Testing only with the build team, who rarely look like the full user base.',
    'Adding accessibility as an afterthought just before release.',
