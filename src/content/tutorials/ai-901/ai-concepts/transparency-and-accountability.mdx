## Start with a question

<Story question="If a self-checkout machine wrongly charges you twice, who do you complain to: the machine, or the shop?">

The shop, obviously. The machine can't apologise or give you a refund. And you'd want the receipt to show exactly what you were charged for.

AI is the same. You need a clear receipt (**transparency**: what it did and why) and a person who owns the outcome (**accountability**: who answers for it). These are the last two of Microsoft's six responsible AI principles.

</Story>

## Explain it like I'm 10

<Eli10>

When you do maths homework, your teacher says "show your working". That's **transparency**: anyone can see how you got the answer and spot a mistake.

And when the homework is handed in, it has **your name** on it. If it's wrong, you fix it, not your pencil. That's **accountability**.

Good AI shows its working, and a real person puts their name on it.

</Eli10>

## The technical explanation

### Transparency

A transparent AI system lets people understand three things:

1. **That AI is involved.** Tell users when they're talking to a bot or reading AI-generated content. Images from gpt-image-1 carry content credentials (C2PA) that record they were AI-generated.
2. **How it reaches results.** Show the sources behind an answer (citations from grounding), or the main factors behind a prediction.
3. **What it can't do.** State known limits, like "may be wrong about recent events".

Microsoft publishes **transparency notes** for its AI services, describing intended uses, how the system works and its limitations. In the Foundry model catalog, every model has a **model card** with its purpose, training notes, benchmarks and limits.

### Accountability

An accountable AI system has people who answer for it. That means:

- A named **owner** for each AI system, and a review before it goes live.
- **Governance**: policies, impact assessments and sign-off for high-risk uses.
- **Human-in-the-loop** review for decisions that significantly affect people.
- **Audit trails**: logs, evaluations and **tracing** so you can find out what happened.
- **Access control** with Microsoft Entra ID roles (for example Foundry Owner and Foundry User), so you know who changed what.
- A route for people to **challenge** a decision.

| Need | Foundry or Microsoft support |
| --- | --- |
| Understand a service's intended uses and limits | Transparency notes |
| Understand a specific model | Model cards in the model catalog |
| Show where an answer came from | Grounding with citations, groundedness detection |
| Know what an agent did, step by step | Tracing, agent versions |
| Prove the system was tested | Evaluation results kept with each release |
| Control who can change it | Foundry RBAC roles |

<Callout type="exam">
"Users must understand how the system made a decision" or "inform users that content is AI-generated" points to **transparency**. "A person must review decisions", "define who is responsible" or "establish governance" points to **accountability**. Remember: you can't make the AI itself accountable. People and organisations are.
</Callout>

<DeepDive title="Explainable enough for whom?">

Large language models can't give you a neat formula for every answer. Transparency there means practical openness: grounding answers in documents and showing citations, disclosing the model and its limits, and keeping traces. For a classic machine learning model that scores loans, you can go further with feature importance (which inputs pushed the score up or down). Pick the level of explanation the affected person actually needs.

</DeepDive>
