keyTerms:
  - term: Responsible AI
    definition: Designing, building and running AI so it's fair, safe, private, inclusive, understandable and has people answerable for it.
  - term: Fairness
    definition: An AI system treats people in similar situations the same way, regardless of gender, ethnicity, age or other traits.
  - term: Reliability and safety
    definition: An AI system behaves as intended, handles unexpected input and fails in a safe way.
  - term: Privacy and security
    definition: An AI system protects personal data, respects consent and resists misuse and attacks.
  - term: Inclusiveness
    definition: An AI system is designed to work for and engage people of all abilities, languages and backgrounds.
  - term: Transparency
    definition: People understand that AI is being used, how it reaches results and what its limitations are.
  - term: Accountability
    definition: Named people and organisations are answerable for how an AI system is built, used and governed.

## Start with a question

<Story question="Would you trust a new doctor who couldn't explain their diagnosis, treated some patients worse than others and shared your records with strangers?">

You wouldn't. A good doctor treats everyone fairly, gets it right or says when they're unsure, keeps your secrets, can talk to you in a way you understand, explains their reasoning and answers to a medical council when things go wrong.

We expect the same from AI. Microsoft writes those expectations down as **six responsible AI principles**, and AI-901 expects you to recognise each one in a scenario.

</Story>

## Explain it like I'm 10

<Eli10>

Imagine a robot referee for your school cricket match. A good robot referee:

- gives **everyone** the same call for the same shot (fair),
- doesn't crash when it rains (reliable and safe),
- doesn't tell the other team your batting plan (private and secure),
- works for kids who can't hear the whistle too (inclusive),
- tells you **why** you're out (transparent),
- and if it makes a bad call, a **teacher** fixes it (accountable).

</Eli10>

## The technical explanation

Microsoft's **responsible AI principles** guide how AI is designed, built and run. Microsoft applies them internally through its Responsible AI Standard, and it builds tools into Microsoft Foundry so customers can apply them too.

| Principle | The question it asks | One example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fairness** | Does it treat similar people the same way? | A hiring model must not score CVs lower because the name sounds female. |
| **Reliability and safety** | Does it work as expected and fail safely? | A medicine chatbot refuses to guess a dose and points to a pharmacist. |
| **Privacy and security** | Is personal data protected, and can attackers misuse it? | A support bot masks phone numbers and Aadhaar numbers before saving chat logs. |
| **Inclusiveness** | Does it work for people of all abilities and backgrounds? | A banking voice app understands Indian English accents and offers captions. |
| **Transparency** | Do people know it's AI and understand how it reaches results? | A loan app tells applicants an AI helped and shows the main reasons for the result. |
| **Accountability** | Who's answerable for what it does? | A named team owns the model, reviews it before launch and handles appeals. |

### Where the principles live in Microsoft Foundry

| Foundry feature | Principles it supports |
| --- | --- |
| Guardrails and content filters (hate, sexual, violence, self-harm, with severity levels) | Reliability and safety |
| Prompt Shields (jailbreak and indirect prompt injection attacks) | Reliability and safety, privacy and security |
| PII detection in Azure Language | Privacy and security |
| Evaluations (quality, plus risk and safety evaluators) | Fairness, reliability and safety |
| Groundedness detection, citations, transparency notes, model cards | Transparency |
| Tracing, role-based access, human review steps | Accountability |

Microsoft also commits that your prompts and completions in Foundry aren't used to train the foundation models. That's a privacy promise you'll see in exam scenarios.

<Callout type="exam">
The exam describes a situation and asks which principle applies. Look for the trigger: different outcomes for different groups is **fairness**, people with disabilities or other languages is **inclusiveness**, "explain the decision" is **transparency**, "who is responsible" or "human oversight" is **accountability**. Remember that reliability and safety is one principle, and privacy and security is one principle.
</Callout>

<DeepDive title="Principles pull against each other">

Real systems trade principles off. Logging every prompt helps accountability but adds privacy risk, so you log with PII masked and limit who can read the logs. Stricter content filters improve safety but can block legitimate medical questions, which hurts inclusiveness for the people who need them. A responsible design writes those trade-offs down, tests them with evaluations and assigns someone to decide.

</DeepDive>
