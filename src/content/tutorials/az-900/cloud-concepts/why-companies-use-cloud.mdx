interview:
  - level: beginner
    q: Give me three reasons a company would move to the cloud.
    a: >-
      First, cost. No big up-front hardware purchase, and you pay for what you use. Second, speed. Servers are ready in minutes instead of weeks. Third, scale. You can handle traffic spikes and expand to new countries without building datacenters.
    followUps:
      - q: Is there any downside?
        a: >-
          Yes. Costs can grow if nobody manages them, you depend on the provider and your internet connectivity, and you still own security for your data and identities. Moving also takes effort and new skills.
  - level: intermediate
    q: How would you explain the business value of cloud to a non-technical manager?
    a: >-
      I would avoid jargon and talk about outcomes. We can launch new ideas in days, we stop paying for idle servers, the website stays up on our busiest days, and our engineers work on features customers see instead of maintaining hardware. Then I would back it with numbers from a pilot or a TCO comparison.
    followUps: []
  - level: experienced
    q: What are the common reasons cloud migrations fail to deliver the expected savings?
    a: >-
      Lift-and-shift without right-sizing, so oversized VMs run 24/7. No tagging or ownership, so nobody is accountable for spend. Ignoring reservations and savings plans for steady workloads. Data egress and cross-region traffic left out of estimates. Keeping old operating models such as manual change tickets, so the agility gains never appear. FinOps practices and architecture changes towards managed services and autoscaling are what actually deliver the savings.
    followUps: []
  - level: scenario
    q: A hospital wants to analyse ten years of anonymised scans with AI but has no GPU servers. What does the cloud offer?
    a: >-
      They can rent GPU capacity only for the weeks the analysis runs, use managed AI services, and store data in a region that meets healthcare compliance requirements. Buying GPU servers for a one-off project would be slow and expensive, and the hardware would sit idle afterwards.

## Start with a question

<Story question="Your online shop gets featured on national TV tonight. Can your website handle 50 times the normal traffic by 8 pm?">

If your website runs on two servers in an office cupboard, the honest answer is no. Ordering more servers takes weeks. By the time they arrive, the TV moment is over and those customers have gone elsewhere.

That single story contains most of the reasons companies move to the cloud: **speed**, **scale**, **cost** and **not losing customers**.

</Story>

## Explain it like I'm 10

<Eli10>

Imagine your class is planning a party. You *could* buy 200 chairs, store them in your classroom all year, and fix them when they break. Or you could **rent chairs** for the party day, get exactly as many as you need, and return them afterwards.

Most companies used to buy all the chairs. The cloud lets them rent instead. They also get to spend their time planning a great party (building their app) rather than repairing chairs (fixing servers).

</Eli10>

## The technical explanation

The business case for cloud usually rests on six benefits. Each one gets its own lesson later, so here is the map:

| Benefit | The business problem it solves | Covered in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost efficiency | Large up-front hardware spend, paying for idle capacity | Consumption-based model, CapEx vs OpEx |
| Speed and agility | Weeks of procurement before any project can start | This lesson |
| Scalability and elasticity | Traffic spikes, growth, seasonal demand | Scalability, Elasticity |
| Availability and reliability | Downtime when hardware or a datacenter fails | High Availability, Reliability |
| Security and governance | Keeping up with threats and compliance rules | Security, Governance |
| Manageability | Too much manual operational work | Manageability |

### Speed is often the hidden winner

Cost gets the headlines, but many organisations say **agility** is the bigger prize. When a developer can create a test environment in five minutes and delete it at lunchtime, the whole organisation experiments more. Ideas that would never justify a hardware purchase can be tried for the price of a coffee.

### Economies of scale

Microsoft buys servers, electricity and network capacity at a scale no single company can match. It passes part of that saving on, which is why compute and storage unit prices tend to fall over time.

### Focus on what makes you different

A bank's customers don't care who replaced a failed disk last night. They care about the banking app. Every hour engineers spend on **undifferentiated heavy lifting**, such as racking servers or patching firmware, is an hour not spent on the product.

<TryIt labs="vm-and-cost" text="Feel the agility: launch a server in seconds, then stop it and watch the cost drop." />

<Callout type="warning">
"Cloud is cheaper" is only true when it's used well. A company that copies its servers to the cloud unchanged and leaves them running 24/7 at full size can pay *more*. Cost benefits come from right-sizing, autoscaling, switching things off and using managed services.
</Callout>
