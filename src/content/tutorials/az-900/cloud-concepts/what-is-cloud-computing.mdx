interview:
  - level: beginner
    q: In one or two sentences, what is cloud computing?
    a: >-
      Cloud computing means using computing services such as servers, storage, databases and software over the internet from a provider like Microsoft, instead of buying and maintaining my own hardware. I create what I need on demand and usually pay only for what I use.
    followUps:
      - q: Can you give me a real-world example?
        a: >-
          Gmail or Outlook.com is cloud software: I never install a mail server. For a business example, an online exam site can rent 50 extra servers during exam week and release them afterwards, paying only for that week.
  - level: intermediate
    q: What are the essential characteristics that make something "cloud" rather than just "a server in someone else's building"?
    a: >-
      The NIST definition lists five: on-demand self-service, broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity and measured service. A rented server you order by email and pay for yearly is hosting, not cloud. Cloud means I can provision it myself through an API in minutes, scale it up or down quickly, and pay based on metered usage.
    followUps: []
  - level: experienced
    q: Cloud is often described as "cheaper". Is that always true?
    a: >-
      No. Cloud changes the cost model from capital expense to operating expense and removes idle capacity, but a steady, always-on workload lifted as-is onto pay-as-you-go VMs can cost more than amortised hardware. The savings come from right-sizing, autoscaling, reservations, managed services that cut operational effort, and speed. I always frame it as total cost of ownership and business agility, not raw unit price.
    followUps:
      - q: So when would you keep a workload on-premises?
        a: >-
          Strict data residency or sovereignty rules that a cloud region can't meet, ultra-low-latency needs next to factory machines, heavy recent hardware investment that isn't yet depreciated, or legacy systems that can't be moved. Often the answer is hybrid rather than all-or-nothing.
  - level: scenario
    q: A school's results website crashes every year on results day. The rest of the year it is almost idle. How would cloud computing help?
    a: >-
      This is the classic spiky workload. On-premises they would have to buy enough servers for results day and leave them idle for 364 days. In the cloud they can scale out automatically for the peak, or use a platform service that autoscales, and scale back in afterwards, paying for the extra capacity only on the busy day.

## Start with a question

<Story question="What happens when 10,000 students try to open the same online exam website at 9:00 sharp?">

If the school runs the site on one server in the staff room, it slows to a crawl and then falls over. Students panic, teachers get angry phone calls, and the IT person is blamed.

The obvious fix is "buy more servers". But exams only happen a few days a year. For the other days, those expensive machines sit there humming, using electricity and needing updates. That is the problem cloud computing was invented to solve.

</Story>

## Explain it like I'm 10

<Eli10>

Think about electricity at home. Your family doesn't own a power station. You plug something in, the power is just there, and at the end of the month you pay for the units you used. If you hold a big party and switch on every light, the power company handles it.

**Cloud computing is the same idea for computers.** Instead of buying your own big computers, you borrow them from a company like Microsoft over the internet. Need ten computers for an hour? You get them. Done? Give them back and stop paying.

</Eli10>

## The technical explanation

**Cloud computing is the on-demand delivery of IT resources over the internet, with pay-as-you-go pricing.** A cloud provider such as Microsoft owns massive datacenters around the world. It rents out what those datacenters can do as services:

- **Compute**: virtual machines, containers, serverless functions. The "brains" that run your code.
- **Storage**: files, blobs (images, videos, backups), disks.
- **Networking**: virtual networks, load balancers, private connections, DNS.
- **Databases**: managed SQL and NoSQL databases.
- **Analytics and AI**: data warehouses, machine learning, AI services.
- **Complete software**: email, office apps, CRM (customer relationship management) delivered ready to use.

What separates real cloud from an ordinary data centre lease is captured in the widely used NIST definition. It has **five essential characteristics**:

| Characteristic | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| On-demand self-service | You create a server yourself in minutes through a portal, command line or API. No ticket, no purchase order. |
| Broad network access | Services are reachable over the network from laptops, phones and other services. |
| Resource pooling | The provider shares huge pools of hardware across many customers, keeping each one logically isolated. |
| Rapid elasticity | Capacity can be added or removed quickly, often automatically. |
| Measured service | Usage is metered per second, per GB or per request, and you're billed accordingly. |

<DeepDive title="What is actually happening under the hood?">

Three technologies make cloud computing possible at this scale:

1. **Virtualisation**: a hypervisor splits one physical server into many isolated virtual machines. Azure runs a customised Hyper-V-based hypervisor on its hosts.
2. **Software-defined everything**: networks, storage and even firewalls are configured by software, which is why you can build an entire network with an API call.
3. **Automation and orchestration**: a control plane (in Azure, Azure Resource Manager) takes your request, places it on suitable hardware, wires it up and tracks it.

For architects the key mental shift is this. Hardware becomes a **programmable, disposable commodity**. You design for failure: assume any single machine can disappear, keep state outside it, and let automation replace it. That is why cloud-native designs lean on stateless services, managed databases and infrastructure as code.

</DeepDive>

## Real-world business scenario

<Scenario
  company="An online learning platform (similar to many edtech start-ups)"
  industry="Online education"
  problem="Traffic is quiet most of the month but jumps 20× on exam and results days. Buying servers for the peak would waste money; buying for the average would crash on exam day."
  service="Cloud-hosted web app with autoscaling, a managed database and blob storage for course videos."
  why="No up-front hardware purchase, capacity that follows demand automatically, and a provider that handles the physical datacenter, power and hardware repairs."
  outcome="The site stays up on exam day, the bill drops on quiet days, and the small team spends its time on features instead of fixing servers."
/>

The same pattern shows up everywhere: a food delivery app at dinner time, a shopping site on sale day, a bank's month-end batch jobs. The business problem is always some mix of "we need capacity fast", "we do not want to pay for idle machines" and "we do not want to run a datacenter".
