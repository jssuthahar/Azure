  - level: beginner
    q: What is high availability?
    a: >-
      High availability means a system keeps working with very little downtime even when parts of it fail. For example, running two web servers behind a load balancer so if one crashes, the other keeps serving users.
    followUps:
      - q: How does Azure describe availability?
        a: >-
          With Service Level Agreements (SLAs) that state a guaranteed uptime percentage for each service, like 99.95% or 99.99%. If Microsoft misses it, customers can claim service credits.
  - level: intermediate
    q: How much downtime does a 99.9% SLA allow per month, versus 99.99%?
    a: >-
      About 43.8 minutes per month for 99.9%, and about 4.4 minutes for 99.99%. Every additional nine cuts allowed downtime by a factor of ten.
    followUps: []
  - level: experienced
    q: Your app uses App Service (99.95%) and Azure SQL Database (99.99%). What is the composite SLA and how would you improve it?
    a: >-
      Both must work, so you multiply, 0.9995 × 0.9999 ≈ 99.94%, which is lower than either part. To raise it, deploy zone-redundant tiers in a region with availability zones, or run active-active in two regions behind Azure Front Door with geo-replicated data. That raises availability because a failure must hit both paths at once. Also remember the SLA is the provider's commitment for its part. Your own code, deployments and dependencies affect real availability too.
    followUps:
      - q: Does a higher SLA always justify the cost?
        a: >-
          No. Start from the business impact of downtime. If an hour of outage costs little, a multi-region active-active design may cost more than it saves. Define availability targets with the business, then design to them.
  - level: scenario
    q: An online exam platform cannot be down during exam windows. What would you design for high availability in Azure?
    a: >-
      At least two instances of each tier spread across availability zones, zone-redundant database and storage, a load balancer or Front Door in front, health probes so unhealthy instances are removed, and deployment slots to avoid downtime during releases. Optionally a secondary region for regional failure.

## Start with a question

<Story question="What happens to an online exam if the only server running it crashes at 9:15?">

Ten thousand students see an error page. Some lose their answers. The school has to reschedule, and trust is gone.

Now imagine the same exam running on **three servers in three separate buildings**. One crashes, and the other two carry on. Students don't even notice. That is **high availability**.

</Story>

## Explain it like I'm 10

<Eli10>

Think about the **lights in your classroom**. If there were just one bulb and it broke, the room would go dark. But there are many bulbs, so if one breaks, you can still see.

High availability is putting **lots of "bulbs"** (copies of a computer program) in **different places**, so if one stops working, the others keep the lights on.

</Eli10>

## The technical explanation

**High availability (HA)** is the ability of a system to remain operational and accessible for as much time as possible. Failures *will* happen: disks die, servers crash, power fails, a datacenter has a problem. HA design makes sure **no single failure takes the service down**.

The key technique is **redundancy**, removing every single point of failure:

| Failure | Azure HA technique |
| --- | --- |
| One VM or instance crashes | Run 2+ instances behind a load balancer (Scale Sets, App Service with multiple instances) |
| A server rack or update domain has a problem | Availability sets spread VMs across fault and update domains |
| An entire datacenter goes down | **Availability zones**: spread instances across physically separate zones |
| A whole region is unavailable | Multi-region deployment with Azure Front Door or Traffic Manager |
| Storage hardware fails | Redundant storage (LRS, ZRS, GRS, GZRS), covered in Module 2 |

### Service Level Agreements

Microsoft publishes an **SLA** for each service: a formal commitment to a percentage of uptime (or connectivity), with **service credits** if it falls short. Small differences in percentages mean very different amounts of downtime. Try it:

<Callout type="exam">
For AZ-900 you don't need to memorise every SLA. Understand that **SLAs are expressed as percentages**, that **higher percentages mean less downtime**, that **combining services multiplies their SLAs** (and lowers the total), and that **redundancy across zones or regions** raises availability.
</Callout>

<DeepDive title="Designing HA without wasting money">

- **Start with targets**: agree on an availability objective with the business (for example 99.95%), plus recovery time and recovery point objectives (RTO, RPO) for disasters.
- **Zone-redundant by default** in regions that support zones. For many PaaS services (App Service Premium, Azure SQL Database, Storage ZRS) it's a configuration flag, often at little or no extra cost.
- **Health probes and graceful degradation**: load balancers should remove unhealthy instances quickly; the app should degrade (for example, read-only mode) rather than fail completely.
- **Deployments cause outages too**: blue-green or slot swaps, feature flags and safe rollout reduce self-inflicted downtime, which is often the biggest source of unavailability.
- **Multi-region is expensive** in money and complexity (data replication, consistency, failover testing). Use it where the business impact justifies it.

</DeepDive>
